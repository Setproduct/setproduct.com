You paid for the placement. The post went live on a Tuesday with a working link, and a `site:` search confirms that Google indexed the page. Six weeks later, Search Console shows 0 clicks and a few dozen impressions for queries you never targeted.

So you type the question into Google: why is my guest post not ranking? The usual answers blame the link, either a low DR or slow indexing.

We run a blog with 17,000 monthly readers, and from the publishing side the picture looks different. The link is almost never the problem. The text answers no query that anyone types into a search bar, so Google has nothing to rank it for.

That is how a guest post with no traffic can sit next to a working link. The link did its job on the day it went live. The page around it never had a job.

Below are seven causes, each with a sign you can check yourself. After them comes the research protocol we run before the first line is written, so you can copy it or hand the work to us.

## Is the guest post not indexed, or indexed and not ranking?

These are two different problems, and the first takes 2 minutes to rule out. Search for `site:` plus the full URL of the post.

If the page is still missing two weeks after publication, the cause sits with the host. Typical ones are a noindex tag, a canonical that points elsewhere, or a site Google crawls rarely because it publishes anything for money. Our list of [red flags in guest post outreach](/blog/red-flags-in-guest-post-outreach) shows how to spot that kind of site before you pay.

If the page is indexed and still gets nothing, keep reading. Some buyers try to fix this with a host that gets crawled faster, such as [a site listed in Google News](/blog/google-news-guest-posts). Faster crawling solves discovery, and discovery was already working.

## Seven reasons your guest post is not ranking

The causes stack, so check all seven signs before you settle on one.

![Seven reasons a guest post is not ranking, grouped by the moment each one is decided, with a one-line sign for every cause](/blog/assets/guest-post-not-ranking/01-seven-causes-timeline.webp)

### ❶ The topic was picked for traffic, with no buyer behind it

A topic with 20,000 monthly searches looks like the safe choice. It attracts readers who want a definition, along with the large sites that already own that definition. A query with 200 searches from people comparing tools is harder to find, and it is the one a buyer types.

**The sign.** Read your target query aloud and ask who types it. If the honest answer is a student or a competitor's content team, the post cannot earn revenue even at position 1.

### ❷ One post tries to answer five queries and answers none

The brief said "cover everything about onboarding", so the text gives one paragraph to each subtopic. Google ranks a page for the query it answers best. A page that spends 150 words on each of five questions answers every one of them worse than a page devoted to a single question.

**The sign.** Search Console lists the post under 30 or more queries, all at positions 40 to 90, and no single query pulls ahead of the rest.

### ❸ Nobody mapped which page on the host owns which query

If the host already has a page ranking for your topic, your post splits impressions with it.

We made this mistake on our own blog. Five of our pages about guest posting competed for the same searches: 430 of 1,647 queries appeared on at least two of them. The cluster sat at average positions 11 to 29.

We have since assigned one query group to each page, and the result is still being measured.

**The sign.** Search `site:` plus the host domain plus your main keyword. If an older page from the same site appears above yours, the two are competing.

### ❹ The post links to nothing else on the host site

A guest post with one outbound link to the buyer and zero links to the host's other articles is an island. Internal links show Google where a page sits inside the site's topics, and they bring readers who are already on the blog.

**The sign.** Count the links in your post that lead to other articles on the same blog. Then count the host articles that link back to yours. Two zeros is your answer.

### ❺ There is no structure AI Overview can lift

For informational queries Google often shows an AI Overview above the regular results. It is assembled from passages, and the passages that get used are short and direct. That means a question in the subheading and a plain answer in the first two sentences under it.

Search Engine Land keeps a running [library of AI Overviews coverage](https://searchengineland.com/library/platforms/google/google-ai-overviews).

**The sign.** Your subheadings are labels like "Benefits" or "Overview", and no paragraph in the post could be quoted alone as an answer.

### ❻ The link points to the homepage instead of a product page

A reader who finishes an article about invoice automation and lands on a generic homepage has to find the invoice feature again. The link should continue the topic of the article on your side, which means a product or feature page.

The anchor should describe that page in plain words. Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#link-spam) list guest posts with keyword-optimized anchor text among the examples of link spam, so write the anchor for the reader.

**The sign.** The anchor is "click here" or a bare brand name, and the target URL is your root domain.

### ❼ Nobody touched the post after it went live

The title and the meta description are still the first draft, and so are the images. Within weeks Search Console shows which queries the page gets impressions for, and they often differ from the plan. A post that is never adjusted to that data keeps its launch-day guesses.

**The sign.** The post has impressions at positions 3 to 10 and a CTR under 1%. People see it and do not click, which is a title and description problem.

☞ The first three causes are decided before a single word is written. No edit after publishing repairs a topic nobody searches.

## Does guest posting still work for SEO?

Yes, when the post is built to rank for something. A guest post gives you two assets. One is the link, and the other is a page on someone else's domain that can hold a position and send readers for years.

When buyers say their guest posts are not working, they usually mean the first asset arrived and the second never did. We listed the outcomes of the channel, including the cases where it fails, in [benefits of guest blogging](/blog/benefits-of-guest-blogging). ## The protocol we run before the first line

Every cause above is a research failure that surfaced after publishing. The [Delegate plan](https://publish.setproduct.com/pricing#delegate) moves that research to the front. It takes seven steps, all finished before writing starts.

Our team researches your product and writes the article. We use AI actively to speed up research and first drafts, and a human checks every fact before the draft reaches you.

The inputs come from your brief, which has fields for the product, the buyer, the goal, your links and your competitors. A separate check after publishing handles the last cause.

![Map of the seven-step research protocol, running from the four groups of the client brief to the query, outline and link target that go to writing](/blog/assets/guest-post-not-ranking/03-research-protocol-map.webp)

### ❶ Product

We start with what you sell and what a reader should do after the article. We read the pricing page and the docs, then pick the one feature the article can show without overstating it.

→ **Example question.** "Which page should a buyer land on, and what do you want them to do there?"

→ **What we do next.** The answer fixes the link target and the anchor before writing starts.

**Closes cause 6.** The link lands on the page that continues the article.

### ❷ Market and competitors

We list who else answers the reader's problem, meaning direct competitors plus the pages that hold the search results today. The brief asks for one to three competitors to mention or avoid, and we follow up with one question about them: which competitor do buyers compare you with most often, and where do you win that comparison? From the answers we mark the queries where strong pages already hold the top results, and the ones where current answers are thin.

### ❸ Audience

"Marketers" is too wide to write for. We want the job title of the buyer and the moment they start searching, in their own words.

→ **Example question.** "What did your last 3 customers type into Google before they found you?"

→ **What we do next.** Their wording becomes the seed list for the keyword map, in place of the vocabulary from your marketing pages.

**Closes cause 1.** The seed words come from buyers, not from marketing pages.

### ❹ Risks

Some claims cannot go into a published article, such as unreleased features or comparisons your legal team has not cleared. We also check the topic against the subjects we turn down: gambling, CBD, adult content, illegal substances, hate speech, spam.

→ **Example question.** "What must this article never say about you or about a competitor?"

→ **What we do next.** The answers become a list we run the draft against before you see it. Anchors written for keywords go on the same list, because Google treats them as link spam.

**Closes the anchor side of cause 6.** The anchor describes the page in plain words.

### ❺ Potential

We weigh search demand against the strength of the pages that rank now, then ask how close the query sits to a purchase. A query where we cannot reach page one is dropped at this step, and you settle one trade-off for us: 2,000 curious readers, or 150 who are choosing a tool this quarter. What survives is a shortlist of 3 to 5 candidate queries, and we discard the rest.

**Closes cause 1, together with step 3.** Only queries a buyer types survive the shortlist.

### ❻ Keyword map

Each candidate query is checked against our own blog. If one of our pages already ranks for it, a second page would compete with the first.

**1.** We list the queries our blog already holds and ask ourselves which gap this article fills.

**2.** Your article gets one primary query with its sub-questions.

**3.** We choose the existing posts that will link to it.

**Closes causes 2, 3 and 4.** One primary query per article, no page on the host competing, and older posts linking in.

### ❼ Topic and angle

Only now do we choose the topic. The angle is the reason someone would read this article when ten others exist.

**1.** We write the title first, then the question subheadings.

**2.** We write the opening answer and test it with one question: if a reader remembers one sentence of this article, which one should it be?

**3.** The outline goes to writing, and the finished draft goes to you for one round of edits.

**Closes cause 5.** Question subheadings with direct answers are in the outline from the start.

### After publishing

This loop starts once the post is live and runs separately from the research. Four to six weeks after publishing, we open Search Console and read the queries the page actually gets. If it sits at positions 3 to 10 with a CTR below 1%, we rewrite the title and the meta description for those queries, free of charge and with no time limit.

**Closes cause 7.** The post stops carrying its launch-day guesses.

This same check at four to six weeks is how we answer for the top 10 goal, so it is included in the price and never sold separately.
